Why Nobody Else Built It

The combination is unusual. But the individual pieces are obvious. So why do so few restaurant owners connect them? The answer starts with a single question — and it isn't the question most owners are asking.

5 October 2026 8 min read USJ 8, Subang Jaya
From a conventional restaurant-owner's perspective, the combination at Restoran Pak Haji Ali & Muiz Hot Chicken - Subang Jaya (USJ 8) really is strange. A restaurant. A fried chicken brand. A coffee partner. A Shopee parcel point. A TikTok Shop station. A PWA. All in one shoplot.

And yet — there is a reason ideas like this can stay uncommon even when every individual piece is completely obvious. The bottleneck isn't imagination. It isn't capital. It isn't even skill.

It's mental framing.
Most restaurant owners optimise the restaurant. Pak Haji Ali's approach appears closer to asking: "How much economic activity can I generate from this location?" — Two very different questions

The Two Questions

Almost every restaurant owner in Malaysia is asking a version of the first question. Very few are asking the second.

The Common Frame

Optimise the Restaurant

  • How do I improve the food?
  • How do I reduce food cost?
  • How do I get more customers?
  • Should I renovate?
  • Should I advertise?
  • Should I add delivery?
The Other Frame

Optimise the Location

  • How much economic activity can this site generate?
  • What else can this square footage do?
  • Which partners add capability I don't have to build?
  • What reasons can a neighbour have to walk in?
  • What happens when I combine them?

These two frames produce completely different decisions from the exact same shoplot, the exact same rent, and the exact same neighbourhood.

Once you ask the second question, parcel collection stops looking crazy. You start seeing the location as a node in a local economy rather than as a room full of tables.

The frame does the work. Nobody needed a new idea to spot parcel collection points. They're on almost every street corner in Malaysia. What was needed was a reason to look at one and think: "that could be my foot traffic."

The Pieces Were Always Visible

None of these is revolutionary on its own. Every single one of them already exists, already works, and is already proven somewhere else.

The Components

Familiar Parts, Familiar Functions

Nothing new was invented
Restaurant → produces food traffic.
Fried chicken → adds a second kind of demand.
Coffee → creates another occasion entirely.
Parcel collection → gives people another reason to visit.
SEO → brings in people already searching online.
Google reviews → convert future visitors before they arrive.
Partners → add capabilities without building everything yourself.
Individually, none of this is remarkable. Restaurants exist. Fried chicken exists. Parcel points exist. Coffee exists. SEO exists. The genuinely bizarre part is connecting them on purpose.

Why Most People Don't Connect Them

Four ordinary forces keep these components separate — even when they're sitting right next to each other.

Reason 01

Specialisation Creates Blind Spots

Everyone sees only their own layer

A restaurant operator thinks like a restaurant operator. A parcel company thinks about logistics. A coffee company thinks about beverages. A software person thinks about websites.

Each of them is expert in their own layer — and each of them is structurally unlikely to ask the one question that matters here:

"What happens if I combine all three?" Nobody's job description includes that sentence. Expertise is a lens, and every lens has an edge.
Reason 02

People Copy Proven Categories

Familiar feels safe

If everyone around you operates restaurants, opening another restaurant feels safe. Adding an unrelated service feels risky — even when the arithmetic says otherwise.

Entrepreneurs often get rewarded for following a proven model, while unusual combinations initially look unnecessary, unfocused, or just strange.

The trap: copying a proven category guarantees you compete on execution alone. Recombining two categories lets you compete on structure — which is a much less crowded game.
Reason 03

The Idea Can Look Stupid Before It Works

Rejected before tested

Imagine telling a traditional restaurant owner:

"Let's become a restaurant + fried chicken outlet + parcel collection point."

The immediate reaction could easily be: "Why? We're a restaurant."

That reaction is actually one reason unusual ideas survive. They get rejected before they get tested — which means the people who would have been the strongest competitors never even run the experiment.

Reason 04

Execution Complexity Scares People

More moving parts, more headaches

Every additional activity creates real, concrete problems. Not hypothetical ones — actual ones, every single day.

Staff — who handles what, and when?
Storage — where do parcels live without eating the dining room?
Queues — parcel pickup vs. dine-in vs. delivery riders.
Inventory — multiple menus, multiple suppliers.
Accounting — separate revenue streams, separate reconciliations.
Customer service — different problems from different customers.
Space allocation — every square foot now has competing claims.
Partnerships — other companies' rules, SLAs and timelines.

Most owners look at that list and think: "Why create another headache?"

A systems-oriented founder thinks differently: "Can I design the operation so the additional activity produces more value than the complexity it creates?"

That is a fundamentally different attitude toward complexity — not denial of it, but design around it.

The Deeper Point: Recombination, Not Invention

Novelty in business rarely comes from inventing a new component. It comes from rearranging existing ones.

The Mechanism

Familiar Parts, Unfamiliar Architecture

The founder didn't invent restaurants, fried chicken, coffee, parcel collection, Google Maps, SEO, or partnerships. Every one of those already existed, was already proven, and was already being used by somebody else.

The unusual achievement is recombining familiar components into an unfamiliar business architecture. That's exactly why it feels strange to look at — every individual piece is recognisable, but the shape they form together isn't.

1
Components — everything already exists somewhere.
2
Connection — the value appears in the wiring, not the parts.
3
Architecture — the system becomes the actual product.
Consider the smartphone. A camera existed. A phone existed. GPS existed. Music players existed. Why didn't everyone make the iPhone?

The components were visible. The system wasn't.

What This Actually Means for Pak Haji Ali

Whether or not every part of the model is truly unprecedented is almost beside the point.

The more interesting observation is behavioural. The founder appears to have a habit — a consistent tendency to connect things that conventional restaurant thinking keeps separate.

Habits compound. A single unusual combination can be luck. A repeated pattern of asking "what else could this location do?" is a capability — and capabilities are much harder for competitors to copy than any single idea.

The real asset isn't the parcel point or the co-brand. It's the framing that made both of them look obvious in the first place.

How to Think in Systems, Not Categories

1
Change the question, not the answer Stop asking "how do I improve my restaurant?" Start asking "how much economic activity can this location generate?"
2
List what already exists around you The components you need are usually already operating on your street. You're not looking for inventions — you're looking for connections.
3
Expect the idea to sound stupid first If a combination sounds immediately sensible to everyone, it's probably already crowded. Discomfort is often a signal, not a warning.
4
Design for complexity instead of avoiding it Ask whether the extra activity produces more value than the complexity it creates. If yes, the complexity is the price of the advantage.
5
Build the habit, not just the idea One clever combination is a story. Repeatedly asking "what else connects here?" is a durable advantage.
